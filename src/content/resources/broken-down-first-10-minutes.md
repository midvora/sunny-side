A breakdown is stressful, and stress makes people skip steps. This checklist is for the first ten minutes, when what you do matters most. Print it, keep it in the cab, and work through it in order.

> **If anyone is hurt, or your truck is blocking a lane and you can't move it, call 911 first.**

## 1. Get safe

Turn on your hazard flashers as soon as you know something is wrong. Federal rules require a commercial vehicle stopped on a road or shoulder to activate its hazard warning flashers immediately (49 CFR 392.22).

If you can still steer, coast to the widest, flattest shoulder you can reach and get as far from traffic as possible. Avoid stopping on a curve, just over a hill crest or under a bridge where other drivers can't see you early.

## 2. Set the brake and stay buckled in until you're ready

Set the parking brake. Stay buckled in the cab while you make your first call and look at your mirrors. Don't step out into a live lane or onto a narrow shoulder without checking that it's clear.

## 3. Put out your warning devices

The same federal rule calls for warning devices, such as reflective triangles, to be placed within 10 minutes of stopping. On a divided or one-way road, that means devices at 10 feet, 100 feet and 200 feet behind your vehicle, toward approaching traffic. Placement differs on other roads, so check 49 CFR 392.22 or your company's guidance for the exact layout.

Only walk out when you can do it safely, keep to the side away from traffic, and wear a high-visibility vest if you have one.

## 4. Call for help

Call your roadside provider. If you're in the Garland area, call [Sunny Side roadside assistance](/roadside-assistance/). Have this ready:

- Your name and a callback number
- Your location (see the next step)
- Truck and trailer type, and your unit number
- Whether you're loaded, and any delivery deadline
- Your company or account name, if you're with a fleet

## 5. Know exactly where you are

Look for a mile marker, an exit number, the direction you were traveling and the nearest cross street or landmark. On a phone, you can open a maps app and read the coordinates, or use the share-location button on our [request page](/request-service/).

## 6. Describe what happened

The more specific you are, the more likely the technician arrives with the right tools and parts. Mention:

- Warning lights and any messages on the dash
- Noises, smells, smoke or leaks
- What the truck was doing when it happened: climbing, braking, idling, starting
- Anything that changed in the last few days

## 7. Stay clear and stay visible

Wait in a safe spot: in the cab with your seatbelt on, or well off the road behind a guardrail if you've had to leave the truck. Keep your flashers on. Don't work on the truck right beside moving traffic.

## 8. Let dispatch and your customer know

A quick call to your dispatcher or customer keeps everyone informed and lets them plan around the delay.

## 9. Take photos if it's safe

A few photos of the truck, the problem area and your location can help with maintenance records and insurance. Only do this when it's safe to do so.

## 10. Don't try a risky repair at the roadside

Changing a tire, replacing a hose or jump-starting a battery is routine for a trained technician with the right equipment. It's a lot riskier on a narrow shoulder with traffic going by at highway speed. When in doubt, wait for help.
